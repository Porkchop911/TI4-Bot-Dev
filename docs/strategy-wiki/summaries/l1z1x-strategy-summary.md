# L1Z1X Mindnet: strategy from SCPT 347

**Draft strategy summary · Luna · SCPT episode 347, “Super L1Z1X Mindnet Guide” (published 2024-04-17)**  
Source transcript: complete ASR transcript, verified provenance in the extraction header; references below use its segment IDs and exact speech times. Ruleset compatibility has not been reviewed. The speakers give six-player advice and discuss Milty-style drafts only where identified. These are podcast recommendations, not rules rulings or measured win-rate results.

## Strategic thesis

L1Z1X is a high-variance faction whose tools are unusually concentrated in invasion, bombardment, fleet pressure, and taking structures. Those strengths do not automatically score points. The hosts describe games where L1 excels when the map, objectives, and table make violence productive, but sees weaker results when those advantages do not fit the revealed scoring environment. Their advice is to build the economic and technological base to threaten force, preserve enough tokens and mobility to act at the right moment, and keep a scoring path in view. **C01** (17:43–18:46; s000256, s000272).

The first selection is the slice. A high-influence slice helps pay for the many activations that a late-game strike may require; a blue skip eases the demanding blue technology route. The speakers say they would hesitate to draft L1 without substantial influence or a blue skip, ideally both. This is a warning about running out of gas, not a hard cutoff. **C02** (1:45:44–1:46:10; s001671, s001676).

## Round-one openings

**Diplomacy** is the safest leading option in the guide’s view. L1’s single five-resource home planet gives the card useful refresh value, and taking the card controls when that refresh occurs. The resources can support research and then production; more plastic on the board makes L1’s later threat credible. If a skip is available, Diplomacy also helps pay for the technology plan. The hosts say they consider it in almost every round, though that is an opinion rather than a universal pick order. **C03** (1:04:17–1:05:09; s000958, s000974).

**Leadership** is a middling alternative when the slice lacks influence or the table is likely to leave Diplomacy available as a follow. Its extra tokens can relieve L1’s weak home influence. But pick order matters: if Diplomacy is taken before L1 and Construction is the remaining card, Leadership may deliver tokens without enough early ships to use them. Judge the card by the actual slice and expected follow, rather than the raw token count. **C04** (1:01:01–1:02:09; s000900, s000920).

**Trade** may be best when the table supports debt-meta and commodity washing. Trade goods solve a central L1 weakness because they can be spent flexibly. Yet L1 starts without a trade ship, so neighbors may need to come to you. The discussion compares a potentially strong Diplomacy refresh against Trade’s replenishment and neighbor payments; it concludes that table cooperation and access change the comparison. Favor Trade when you can reliably convert it into trade goods, while recognizing that Diplomacy can produce more immediate raw resources in some round-one positions. **C05** (1:09:45–1:10:57; s001050, s001073).

The guide discourages defaulting to **Warfare** simply to expand farther. L1 already begins with capacity for its opening two-carrier-equivalent force, and the hosts prefer a concentrated home build that creates a stronger round-two fleet and round-three threat. Warfare can still be right when a particular map or objective makes reach more valuable than fleet quality. **C06** (1:12:30–1:13:34; s001100, s001122).

**Technology** is the higher-ceiling opening when funds and a follow make double research feasible. It addresses L1’s awkward starting colors and makes its kit work better, but requires an unusually large investment. The hosts contrast that upside with a reliable but lower-ceiling opening; they do not claim every L1 should take Technology or that taking its secondary is free. **C07** (1:13:42–1:15:05; s001123, s001143).

## Technology and economy

The no-skip route centers on **Gravity Drive**, **Super Dreadnought II**, and **Carrier II**. Gravity Drive improves flagship reach; the dreadnought upgrade supports the faction’s defining ships; Carrier II makes better use of the home dock’s production. The hosts acknowledge that this line consumes many rounds of research and should be adapted to actual tempo. A yellow skip can instead support an early Cruiser II detour before returning to the dreadnought route. This is a situational option when the cruiser has a job and research is affordable, not a default extra upgrade. **C08** (1:18:20–1:19:06; s001209, s001215). **C10** (1:21:32–1:22:24; s001256, s001272).

With a blue skip, the hosts prefer using it to get Gravity Drive early, then considering AI Development Algorithm as a way to reach Super Dreadnought II or Carrier II. They encourage using the skip repeatedly when the game’s research tempo supports it. These are strategic route suggestions; this summary does not verify the current prerequisite rules for each line. **C09** (1:24:21–1:25:15; s001304, s001316).

The speakers strongly discourage routine **Inheritance Systems** research. They consider the two-yellow prerequisite route and the need to exhaust it and spend two resources at research too expensive relative to the effect. That is a forceful podcast judgment, not a rules or value ruling; unusual technology tempo or a particular objective could change an individual player’s calculation. **C11** (39:16–40:26; s000590, s000615).

The economic constraint is larger than a shortage of spendable resources. L1 has a five-resource home system and strong production, but only two commodities and a promissory note whose sale value the hosts openly dispute. One speaker treats it as roughly one trade good, another would pay more in a desperate situation. The guide advises treating the note as a useful one-time sale rather than a repeat income stream, while not expecting it to repair the economy by itself. It also notes that L1’s faction technologies have little synergy with its starting green and red technologies, making research feel even more costly. **C05** (Trade comparison above; s001050–s001073). **C07** (technology timing above). The note’s exact price remains contested in the episode; no fixed price is asserted here.

## Turning combat into advantage

L1’s starting Super Dreadnought and carrier can move an opening two-carrier, four-infantry force, giving the faction a workable initial expansion. The five-resource home planet supports a powerful production step, though its lack of influence remains a real limitation. The guide favors getting useful plastic onto the board rather than assuming the opening fleet alone will win anything. **C14** (30:24–30:48; s000458, s000463).

**Assimilate** makes an invasion more valuable when the target planet contains an enemy PDS or space dock: the host says the structure becomes an L1 unit before the tactical action’s production step, potentially letting the invader build there immediately. This is a concrete reason to value a structured planet, but it requires winning control and having resources, production capacity, and timing to use the opportunity. **C12** (28:24–28:50; s000429, s000434).

**Harrow** applies ships’ bombardment after each ground-combat round. The hosts describe it as a powerful way to swing close invasions, especially with Super Dreadnoughts in the active system. Treat it as extra pressure that can decide a close fight, not a guarantee: the system must contain ships with bombardment and the invasion must still be worth making. **C13** (29:08–30:29; s000444, s000458).

The faction flagship **0.0.1** puts pressure on opposing fleets by requiring its and L1 dreadnought hits in the system to be assigned to non-fighter ships when possible. The hosts recommend integrating the flagship into the larger plan because it can remove high-value ships and reduce the efficiency of fleets that rely on fighters to protect their hitters. They also note that fighters still absorb hits and that some compositions, such as Letnev’s, resist this particular pressure. Use the flagship for the matchup it improves, rather than treating its presence as a guaranteed fleet win. **C15** (31:06–31:44; s000470, s000477).

The mech’s bombardment text prompts an explicit caution in the discussion: once committed to a planet, the mech cannot continue bombarding. The hosts generally value its ground-combat presence and sustain more than leaving it in space to preserve another bombardment source. Decide based on the actual invasion; the episode itself explores edge cases and this summary does not settle them as rules. **C16** (32:26–33:23; s000488, s000503).

The hero, **The Helmsman**, offers a dramatic reposition of the flagship and any number of dreadnoughts into a system without other players’ ships. The speakers value this as a way to create a threat or set up an attack, while warning that opponents can occupy strategically important empty systems and limit destinations. The hero, flagship, and dreadnought package can therefore reward advance planning and visible threat projection. **C17** (47:43–48:57; s000716, s000735).

## Diplomacy, timing, and scoring

The hosts distinguish credible leverage from extortion. A visible fleet can make L1’s offers more persuasive, but threats for payment that does not advance a real goal may damage relationships. They prefer equitable arrangements tied to points or useful exchanges. A large invasion can be justified for a point, an objective, or a meaningful board position; a strike that only eliminates a rival may leave L1 defending an exposed fleet and unable to score afterward. Preserve enough mobility and political goodwill to convert force into a later win. **C18** (1:31:02–1:33:22; s001406, s001441). **C19** (1:33:30–1:34:31; s001442, s001458).

The guide’s preferred posture is to stay in the scoring pack while keeping force available for a decisive late-game “winslay.” It describes an ideal L1 position as third or fourth before the final strike, with enough command tokens and movement to affect the relevant systems. Stopping a leader can be useful, but only if L1 also has a path to score; preventing another player from winning is not itself a victory plan. **C20** (1:35:02–1:35:58; s001464, s001481).

L1 is not naturally strong at bonus points, agendas, economy objectives, or custodians in the hosts’ assessment. Its combat strength also does not equal control: planets must be held and enough tokens retained for activations. The guide suggests a possible late pivot into structure objectives by taking opposing structures with Assimilate, rather than spending early tokens and resources on Construction. That pivot is conditional: opponents’ structures must be reachable and capturable, and point-blocking can create political costs. **C21** (1:37:50–1:38:40; s001514, s001530). **C22** (1:38:41–1:39:01; s001531, s001542).

Mecatol Rex and Imperial can form a catch-up line, but the guide warns against assuming that merely taking Mecatol scores a public objective or enables another score in status. The plan depends on which objectives are revealed, qualification, secrets, and sufficient tempo. If scoring requirements do not line up, taking Mecatol may still help, but it can also consume scarce tokens and attention. Treat each objective’s actual prerequisites separately from partial preparation. **C23** (1:39:22–1:41:32; s001549, s001582).

The episode connects starting Plasma Scoring and bombardment to combat-oriented secrets such as **Turn Their Fleets to Dust** and **Make an Example of Their World**. This is an opportunity, not automatic scoring: the board and the specific objective’s timing conditions still matter. The speakers explicitly discuss a wording change and say Harrow’s later bombardment is not the same as the bombardment step for Make an Example; do not use the general combat synergy to erase that distinction. **C24** (1:41:33–1:42:12; s001583, s001598).

If the public objectives push the game toward stage-two tempo, the hosts see potential in L1’s reach for suitable late control or structure objectives, including distant territories and some single-target objectives. Tokens and actual objective requirements remain the constraint; even combat-oriented L1 may lack enough influence to activate all the systems its plan requires. **C25** (1:42:52–1:44:10; s001616, s001642).

## Counters and draft context

Opponents can blunt L1 by denying clean empty systems for the hero, defending valuable structures, avoiding unnecessary fleet exposure, and forcing L1 to spend activations without scoring. The guide’s own counterplay implications are conditional rather than a blanket prescription: occupying a hero destination matters only if that system would otherwise support L1’s plan, and denying a structure matters most when Assimilate or an objective makes it valuable. L1’s best response is to keep enough economic and token flexibility to change targets rather than commit everything to one visible invasion. **C12, C17, C20, C22**.

For Milty-style drafts specifically, the hosts propose taking L1 around third or fourth to seek a high-influence slice, then accepting a later speaker position from which Diplomacy may still be available. Their logic is that L1 is not usually an early faction pick and can remain viable with later speaker order. This advice assumes that draft order, slice options, and card availability cooperate; it is not presented as a universal map-making recommendation. **C26** (1:47:34–1:48:21; s001696, s001708).
